\title[Reliability of machine learning for magnetic materials]{Label provenance, physical descriptors and chemical novelty constrain machine-learning predictions of magnetic materials}

\abstract{Machine-learning models of magnetic materials are usually judged by their accuracy on held-out data from their training sources. Whether their predictions remain meaningful depends, however, on what the labels represent, on what physical descriptors add and on how far a material lies from the training chemistry. Here we examine these three conditions on one curated dataset of magnetic ordering, Curie temperature and computed magnetic moment, using matched descriptor comparisons, conformal uncertainty and an audited external benchmark. Two thirds of the experimentally antiferromagnetic compounds were ferromagnetic in a large computed database, even for the same space group and in cells that can hold antiferromagnetic order; measured magnetic structures matched the experimental labels. Structural and electronic descriptors from density functional theory gave no detectable gain over composition for ordering or Curie temperature, and their small gain for the moment largely reflected cell size. Marginal calibration hid failures for antiferromagnets, ferrimagnets and the highest Curie temperatures, which class-conditional and normalized methods largely repaired. Error grew with chemical distance across source papers, and in unseen chemical systems performance was much weaker, with wide uncertainty. Reliable use therefore requires provenance-consistent labels, matched tests of added physics and abstention away from familiar chemistry.}

\keywords{magnetic materials, machine learning, Curie temperature, magnetic ordering, data provenance, uncertainty quantification, conformal prediction, distribution shift}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Magnetic materials underpin electric motors, generators and data storage\cite{gutfleisch2011magnetic,coey2010magnetism}, and predicting their magnetic behaviour from composition is a long-standing goal. It is difficult because magnetism is a collective property. Whether a compound orders ferromagnetically (FM), antiferromagnetically (AFM) or ferrimagnetically (FiM), or remains non-magnetic (NM), depends on exchange interactions that are set by the electronic structure and by the local geometry: the distances, coordination and bond angles between magnetic ions, and therefore the crystal symmetry and structural phase\cite{coey2010magnetism}. Competing magnetic states can lie close in energy, so a small change of structure, stoichiometry or disorder can change the ground state. Measured and computed quantities also differ: a Curie temperature \Tc{} belongs to a particular, possibly doped or thin-film sample at finite temperature, whereas a density functional theory (DFT) calculation gives the moment and ordering of one ideal cell at 0~K, among the magnetic configurations that the workflow tried\cite{horton2019ordering}. Composition alone therefore need not determine a magnetic property uniquely, and descriptors that are appropriate in principle may still miss the information that matters.

Large databases have nonetheless made data-driven models possible: high-throughput DFT databases\cite{jain2013materials,saal2013oqmd,choudhary2020jarvis,curtarolo2012aflow}, databases mined from the literature\cite{kelly2025curie,court2020phase,itani2025nemad} and curated collections of magnetic structures\cite{gallego2016magndata}. Models that use only the composition report \Tc{} errors of 56--69~K and ordering accuracies of 86--91\%\cite{nelson2019curie,long2021ferro,belot2023curie,itani2025nemad,abediorang2026curie}, a network that reads atomic coordinates classified ordering with 78\% accuracy\cite{merker2022classifiers}, and other graph networks\cite{choudhary2021alignn} and high-throughput DFT workflows\cite{horton2019ordering,frey2020oxides} offer further routes. Three questions are often conflated when such models are evaluated. Can a model predict held-out observations accurately? Does additional physical information genuinely improve the prediction? Can the model generalize to physically or chemically different materials? A positive answer to the first does not answer the other two.

Each issue has been studied in materials informatics. Random splits overstate the ability to extrapolate\cite{meredig2018extrapolation,xiong2020forwardcv,li2023critical,li2024mdhit,omee2024ood}, and standardised grouped splits now exist\cite{witman2025matfold}; redundancy and leakage between training and test data inflate reported performance\cite{kapoor2023leakage,dunn2020matbench}; accuracy on a proxy target need not carry over to the task of interest\cite{bartel2020stability}; and uncertainty estimates need their own validation\cite{tran2020uq,varivoda2023uq,palmer2022calibration,hu2022conformal}. For magnetic materials, the reported accuracies come from random or stratified splits of a single dataset (Supplementary Table~9), and the reliability of labels has been examined mainly within one source, for example as the precision of a text-mined database\cite{court2020phase} or as the share of DFT entries computed in ferromagnetic configurations\cite{horton2019ordering}, and ab initio predictions of magnetic structures have been benchmarked against measured structures\cite{huebsch2021benchmark}. None of the machine-learning studies of magnetic properties that we examined compared the computed and experimental ordering labels that they trained on for the same compounds, measured the added value of DFT descriptors on identical materials, or tested on chemistry grouped by system or drawn from another label source; in the one cross-dataset test that we found, the \Tc{} error rose from 69 to 90~K\cite{belot2023curie}. What remains unresolved is how consistent the labels of different sources are, how much information physical descriptors add beyond composition, and how quickly reliability decays with chemical novelty, measured together on the same magnetic data and models.

Here we ask under which conditions machine-learning predictions of magnetic properties can be regarded as reliable. We assembled one curated dataset of magnetic ordering, \Tc{} and the DFT magnetic moment that records the source of every label, and evaluated models with and without added DFT descriptors on identical materials, with repeated formula-grouped splits, conformal uncertainty, held-out chemical systems and an audited external benchmark. Our central finding is that the reliability of these predictions depends on the physical consistency and provenance of the target labels, on the information that physical descriptors genuinely add and on the chemical domain in which the predictions are applied, and that accuracy within the training distribution reveals none of the three. Two thirds of the experimental antiferromagnets were ferromagnetic in the computed database, whatever the phase match; structural and electronic DFT descriptors gave no detectable gain for ordering or \Tc{}, and their small gain for the moment mostly reflected cell size; calibration that held on average failed for antiferromagnets, ferrimagnets and the highest \Tc{} values; and error grew with chemical distance, with much weaker performance and wide uncertainty in unseen chemical systems.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Computed and experimental labels describe magnetic order differently}\label{sec:provenance}

We merged seven files from five providers into one schema that records the source of every row (507,828 rows; Methods): Materials Project\cite{jain2013materials}, OQMD\cite{saal2013oqmd}, JARVIS-DFT\cite{choudhary2020jarvis}, three files from NEMAD\cite{itani2025nemad} and the ChemDataExtractor Curie and N\'eel database\cite{kelly2025curie}; measured magnetic structures from MAGNDATA\cite{gallego2016magndata} served as a separate reference. Two of the NEMAD files are its literature tables of magnetic and of anisotropy properties, which a language-model pipeline extracted from journal articles\cite{zhang2024gptarticleextractor}; we call them the experimental compilations. `Experimental' here therefore means values reported from experiments in the literature and extracted automatically, not values that we measured or checked. Written curation rules, which only remove rows, defined three primary targets (Table~\ref{tab:dataset}): magnetic ordering (79,125 records), \Tc{} (29,740) and the total magnetic moment of the DFT cell at 0~K (\mcell{}; 87,513), a computed quantity that is not an experimental saturation magnetization. The targets differ in where their labels come from (Fig.~\ref{fig:provenance}a). The \Tc{} labels come from the literature, 69\% from experimental compilations and 31\% mined from text; the moment comes from DFT for all but 10 of its records; and the ordering labels mix the two kinds of source, 78\% from Materials Project and 22\% from experimental compilations. The class balance differs by provenance as well: 52\% of the Materials Project labels are NM and 2\% AFM, whereas 29\% of the experimental labels are AFM and almost none NM (Fig.~\ref{fig:provenance}b). Curation removed labels that did not mean what their column said, such as N\'eel, superconducting and ferroelectric temperatures recorded as Curie temperatures and 15,493 default `ferromagnetic' labels written by our JARVIS collector (Methods); 11--27\% of the remaining \Tc{} labels were doubtful in a title-based screen (Supplementary Table~5).

Do the two kinds of ordering label describe the same physical state? For 949 formulas, both Materials Project and an experimental source supply a label. They agreed for 28\% of these formulas, with Cohen's $\kappa=0.00$ ($-0.03$ to $0.03$) (Fig.~\ref{fig:provenance}c). Because the two sources have very different class balances, $\kappa$ could not have exceeded 0.34, and the labels are associated (Cram\'er's $V=0.20$) but shifted: when Materials Project calls a compound AFM, the experimental label agrees in 86\% of cases, whereas 336 of 508 experimental antiferromagnets (66\%) are FM in Materials Project (Supplementary Tables~12 and 25). The two labels might refer to different phases, so we compared them where phases can be matched (Methods; Supplementary Table~17). When an experimental record reports a space group equal to that of a Materials Project entry of the same composition, and the label of that entry is used (330 formulas), agreement was 25\% ($\kappa=0.01$; $-0.03$ to $0.06$), and restricting further to bulk, stoichiometric compounds (314 formulas) changed nothing ($\kappa=0.02$; $-0.02$ to $0.06$). Among the phase-matched formulas, 71\% of the experimental antiferromagnets are FM in Materials Project (Fig.~\ref{fig:provenance}d), and formulas whose space groups differ agreed just as poorly (28\%; difference $-0.03$, $-0.09$ to $0.04$). A mismatch of crystal structure, at least at the level of the space group, therefore does not explain the disagreement.

Measured magnetic structures show which convention matches experiment. MAGNDATA\cite{gallego2016magndata,gallego2016magndata2}, hosted by the Bilbao Crystallographic Server\cite{aroyo2011bcs,aroyo2006bcs1,aroyo2006bcs2}, collects magnetic structures determined mainly by neutron diffraction; we labelled each ordered, collinear structure from its own moments (840 compositions; Methods). The experimental labels agreed with these structures for 92\% of 442 shared compositions ($\kappa=0.72$; 0.62 to 0.80; a labeller that always answers AFM would reach 86\%), and the Materials Project labels for 22\% of 251 ($\kappa=0.03$; 0.00 to 0.07); of 218 compounds that are AFM in their measured structure, Materials Project labels 125 (57\%) FM (Fig.~\ref{fig:provenance}d; Supplementary Table~12). The experimental compilations and MAGNDATA draw on the same literature, so this shows that the experimental labels reproduce published structure determinations, not that the two are independent.

Three candidate mechanisms can be partly separated (Supplementary Table~25). A cell with a single magnetic site cannot hold collinear antiferromagnetic order, and all 42 experimental antiferromagnets in such Materials Project cells are FM; but in cells with two or more magnetic sites, where that order can be represented, 72\% of 391 experimental antiferromagnets are still FM. Measured structures whose order needs a magnetic supercell agree with Materials Project about as rarely as those that fit the crystal cell (18\% and 26\%), so neither cell size nor supercells explain most of the disagreement. Part of the FM/FiM confusion comes from the labelling rule, which calls a structure FiM whenever moments of opposite sign occur, including small induced ones: counting only moments of at least 0.5~\muB{} turns about half of the FiM labels into FM (204 to 99). Among the 124 formulas that are FM in experiment and FiM in Materials Project, light rare earths (La--Eu), for most of which (Ce--Sm) the total 4f moment is parallel to the transition-metal moment\cite{coey2010magnetism}, are more common than heavy ones (54 and 33). Even after the recount, agreement is only 35\% ($\kappa=0.05$; 0.02 to 0.08), and the remaining disagreement is concentrated in experimental antiferromagnets labelled FM. That is the pattern expected from a high-throughput workflow that starts from ferromagnetic configurations and enumerates antiferromagnetic orderings for only part of the database\cite{horton2019ordering}, in which 93\% of the magnetic entries were in a ferromagnetic configuration; whether a given entry was included in such a search is not recorded per entry, so this could not be tested directly. The two sources thus encode different conventions for the magnetic ground state, and for the compounds covered by measured structures the experimental convention is the one that matches them. This does not show that a given DFT calculation is wrong, because a computed label refers to the lowest-energy configuration among those tried, at 0~K, in an ideal cell.

Models do not bridge the two conventions (Supplementary Table~21). A model trained on Materials Project labels and tested on experimental labels reached a balanced accuracy of 0.337 (0.273--0.396), indistinguishable from models trained on permuted labels (median 0.342) and far below the 0.769 of a model trained on experimental labels; its recall for antiferromagnets was 7\%. Its test compositions were no farther from the training data than within Materials Project, so the failure lies in the labels and not in the chemistry; the reverse direction reached 0.384 (0.366--0.401), just above the permuted-label models. A pooled ordering score therefore measures agreement with a labelling convention as much as the ability to predict magnetic order: on test compounds with a measured magnetic structure, a model trained on the pooled labels matched its Materials Project labels for 55\% of rows but the measured structures for only 40\% (Supplementary Table~29). The DFT descriptors that we test next come from the same databases, which raises the question of whether they add information about the measured properties or only about the computed ones.

\subsection{Structural and electronic DFT descriptors add little information beyond composition}\label{sec:rq1}

Within the training distribution, composition alone was accurate. For ordering, XGBoost\cite{chen2016xgboost} reached a macro-averaged F1 score (macro-F1) of 0.727 (0.711--0.744) (majority class 0.151). For \Tc{}, gradient boosting\cite{ke2017lightgbm,pedregosa2011sklearn} reached a mean absolute error (MAE) of 76.2~K (69.9--82.5~K) and a coefficient of determination $R^2$ of 0.78 (always predicting the training mean: 218.8~K); for \mcell{} it reached 2.58~\muB{} per cell ($R^2$ 0.70). The weak ordering classes were AFM and FiM (mean recalls over ten splits near 0.55 and 0.49; Supplementary Table~7). Predictors form three nested tiers: 65 composition descriptors (Tier~1), 8 oxidation-state descriptors (Tier~2) and 24 structural and electronic DFT properties of the most stable computed polymorph of each formula (Tier~3), such as the formation energy, band gap, symmetry, volume, density and elastic and dielectric constants. The magnetic outputs of DFT were kept out of Tier~3, because they are the source of the moment labels and of part of the ordering labels; the other properties come from the same spin-polarised, mostly ferromagnetic calculations, so they are not free of magnetism.

To test whether Tier~3 adds information, we compared the tiers on matched populations: the materials with complete Tier~3 descriptors, used with identical rows, splits, hyperparameter grids and metrics at every tier, over ten prespecified formula-grouped splits (Fig.~\ref{fig:rq1}, Table~\ref{tab:tiers}). Intervals allow for refitting\cite{nadeau2003inference}, and the nine contrasts between Tier~1 and Tier~3 are Holm-corrected\cite{holm1979simple} (stability rule in Methods). For ordering and \Tc{}, no algorithm showed a stable effect of Tier~2 or Tier~3, and every adjusted $P$ value was 1.0. For \Tc{} the Tier~3 point estimates were losses: $-14.3$~K ($-42.5$ to $+13.9$) for gradient boosting, $-8.2$~K ($-48.3$ to $+32.0$) for XGBoost and $-14.4$~K ($-33.5$ to $+4.8$) for ridge regression with clipped inputs. Pooled over the three algorithms the change was $-12.3$~K ($-32.7$ to $+8.1$; worse in eight of ten splits), so the average algorithm gains less than our 10-K margin of practical relevance. A 64-setting hyperparameter search for gradient boosting also found no gain ($-11.8$~K; $-35.2$ to $+11.6$; Table~\ref{tab:tiers}). The design detects only large effects, of 27--56~K for \Tc{} and 0.04--0.09 in macro-F1 for ordering, and the intervals leave room for \Tc{} gains of up to 5--32~K for single algorithms. We therefore report no detectable gain; we do not claim that a gain is absent. The matched \Tc{} population, which holds only materials with a full DFT record, is also harder (Tier~1 MAE 203~K, $R^2$ 0.35) and concentrated: 4,602 records from 521 formulas, five of which hold 29\% of the records. Weighting every formula equally did not change the result (gradient boosting $-5.3$~K, $-27.4$ to $+16.8$; Supplementary Table~26), and label noise is not the limit: predicting each test record from the other records of its formula gives an error of 66~K, against 204~K for the model on the same records.

For \mcell{}, Tier~3 gave gains that were stable but small: $+0.079$~\muB{} per cell ($+0.030$ to $+0.127$) for gradient boosting and $+0.062$ ($+0.023$ to $+0.101$) for XGBoost, or 3--4\% of the MAE (adjusted $P=0.046$). Most of this gain is structural normalization and not new information. A single descriptor, the number of sites in the DFT cell, recovered about 70\% of the gradient-boosting gain when it was added to Tier~2, and what Tier~3 added beyond it was not resolved for any algorithm ($+0.023$~\muB{} per cell, $-0.014$ to $+0.061$, for gradient boosting; Fig.~\ref{fig:rq1}c). The moment per formula unit (\mfu{}) behaved in the same way: its Tier~3 gains were stable for all three algorithms but not resolved beyond the site count for the two tree models, whereas ridge regression kept a stable $+0.030$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:rq1}d; Supplementary Table~8). Per atom, a modest gain of 2.8--6.0\% of the MAE remained (Methods). A site count can help even an intensive target because it is a weak proxy for magnetic order: cells that hold several formula units more often carry AFM labels and have lower moments per formula unit (Spearman $\rho=-0.16$; Supplementary Table~15). Tier~3 holds both the cell volume and the volume per atom, which together give the site count, and antiferromagnetic ground states are stored in magnetic supercells, so cell size partly encodes the computed magnetic configuration.

Adding magnetic physics did not change the result for \Tc{} (Supplementary Table~18). Ten descriptors of the magnetic sublattice computed from the crystal structure, including the metal--anion--metal angles that govern superexchange, four magnetic outputs of DFT and a fixed 64-dimensional crystal embedding from the pretrained interatomic potential CHGNet\cite{deng2023chgnet} gave estimates between $-10.8$ and $+9.5$~K against Tier~1, and every interval included zero. Feature attribution would have suggested otherwise: TreeSHAP\cite{lundberg2017shap,lundberg2020treeshap} assigned 40\% (ordering) and 36\% (\Tc{}) of the mean absolute attribution of the Tier~3 XGBoost models to DFT descriptors that did not improve accuracy. Correlated composition and DFT features carry the same information, so attribution shows that a feature is used and not that it adds information. Tier~3 helped neither on Materials Project nor on experimental ordering labels (Supplementary Table~26). Energy differences between magnetic configurations, from which ordering and \Tc{} follow in standard theory, were not available and were not tested, and the sublattice geometry was tested for \Tc{} only.

Tier~3 also has practical limits for screening: it exists only after a DFT calculation, and for ordering and \Tc{} it describes a computed polymorph, not necessarily the measured phase; where the source reports the same space group (536 \Tc{} records), Tier~3 still gave no gain ($-10.9$~K; $-23.8$ to $+2.0$; Supplementary Table~16). Point predictions that are equally accurate with or without added physics leave open whether their uncertainty can be trusted, which we examine next.

\subsection{Marginal calibration conceals material-dependent failures}\label{sec:rq2}

A useful prediction carries an uncertainty that holds for the materials of interest and not only on average. We compared three conformal methods\cite{vovk2005algorithmic,lei2018distribution,angelopoulos2023gentle} at a nominal coverage of 90\%: split conformal prediction, which gives intervals of constant width; normalized conformal prediction, which scales the width by a learned difficulty model\cite{papadopoulos2011normalized}; and conformalized quantile regression (CQR)\cite{romano2019cqr}. Calibration used whole formulas, so the guarantee refers to exchangeable formula groups and not to individual records, and about 4,200 calibration rows act like only 250--340 independent ones (Methods; Supplementary Table~6). On average all three methods met the nominal coverage, with 89.3--90.3\% for \Tc{} and 90.0--90.1\% for \mcell{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:uq}a,b).

Within physically defined subgroups, defined without reference to any method's difficulty score, they did not. Among the highest tenth of predicted \Tc{} values, which contain the materials most relevant to applications, constant-width intervals covered 65.5\% and quantile-regression intervals 68.6\%, against 87.4\% for normalized intervals. For text-mined labels the figures were 78--79\% against 86.0\%, and for the highest predicted third of \mcell{} 72.3\% and 83.4\% against 86.7\%. The normalized intervals were also narrower on average (451~K for \Tc{}, against 569~K), but not best everywhere (Supplementary Tables~11 and 19). Coverage was not protected against a change of label source: intervals calibrated on the larger experimental compilation covered 77--85\% of text-mined records on one frozen split.

For ordering, the failure follows the magnetic classes. After correction to the training prior\cite{saerens2002adjusting} and temperature scaling\cite{guo2017calibration}, the expected calibration error of the class-weighted classifier fell from 0.064 to 0.014 (Supplementary Table~20). Marginal conformal prediction sets then met the nominal coverage overall (89.8\%) by covering ferromagnets (96.2\%) and non-magnets (97.7\%) too often, and antiferromagnets (57.0\%) and ferrimagnets (54.3\%) too rarely (Fig.~\ref{fig:uq}c). The average hid the two classes on which the label sources disagree most. Class-conditional sets\cite{vovk2013conditional} covered 89.8--90.9\% of every class, at the cost of larger sets (1.61 classes on average, against 1.39). These guarantees assume that test materials resemble the calibration materials, and we next ask what happens when they do not.

\subsection{Reliability decays with chemical novelty}\label{sec:rq3}

We distinguish three kinds of novelty: a new formula, a new chemical system (set of elements) and a new source paper. Within our data, holding out whole chemical systems instead of formulas lowered the ordering macro-F1 from 0.712 to 0.684, a change of $-0.028$ ($-0.041$ to $-0.016$) that had the same sign in all ten paired splits and fell most heavily on ferrimagnets; the MAE rose by 0.073~\muB{} per cell ($-0.009$ to $+0.154$) for \mcell{} and by 2.7~K ($-16.8$ to $+22.2$) for \Tc{}, changes that were not resolved (Supplementary Table~21).

For an external test we used a public table of 2,500 experimental Curie temperatures that was curated for an independent study\cite{malaei_magnetic_data,abediorang2026curie}, and scored the Tier~1 XGBoost model for \Tc{} and its normalized intervals without retraining. Independence had to be established record by record (Methods; Supplementary Table~1). By composition, 1,998 records (80\%) were already in our repository, and 1,200 (48\%) carried a value within 0.5~K of one that we held; on these the model scored $R^2$ 0.90, which measures recall and not prediction. After 105 records were excluded for stated reasons, 2,395 remained, of which 465 had compositions that the model had never seen. The table as a whole is therefore not an independent validation; only this blind subset is.

On the 465 blind records, from 81 clusters of source papers, the MAE was 102.7~K (75.1--143.6~K), $R^2$ was 0.69 (0.43--0.82) and the intervals covered 89.5\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:novelty}a,b; Table~\ref{tab:external}). Blind compositions in chemical systems that occur in the training data scored an MAE of 90.6~K and $R^2$ of 0.78. The 69 records in unseen chemical systems, from only 18 clusters, scored 171.9~K (82.6--293.7~K) and $R^2$ of $-0.17$ ($-1.02$ to $0.64$). Performance in unseen chemical systems was thus much weaker, with wide uncertainty: the $R^2$ difference from blind records in seen systems was $-0.95$ ($-1.69$ to $-0.06$), whereas the MAE difference, $+81$~K ($-27$ to $+165$), was not resolved (Supplementary Table~2). Our prespecified condition for claiming no skill, an upper $R^2$ limit of at most 0.2, is not met (0.64, and 0.21 with chemical systems as the resampling unit; Supplementary Table~4). Skill in unseen chemical systems is therefore not established, and neither is its absence. Leaving out any one of the 18 clusters kept $R^2$ between $-0.31$ and 0.14 (Supplementary Table~27); the largest cluster, 17 records from one paper on high-entropy alloys, carries much of the error, and the Curie temperatures of such alloys depend on processing and phase separation that composition cannot describe.

Error followed a similarity measure fixed in advance, the composition distance to the nearest training formula. On the blind records, error rose with distance (Spearman's $\rho=0.28$; 0.10--0.49), and the MAE grew from 62~K in the closest quartile to 165~K in the farthest (Fig.~\ref{fig:novelty}c; Supplementary Fig.~1). The normalized intervals widened from 388 to 529~K, but not enough: coverage fell from 0.95 to 0.85 (Fig.~\ref{fig:novelty}d; Supplementary Table~3). Distance does not explain everything, because blind records in seen systems at comparable distances kept an $R^2$ of 0.75, so a new combination of elements is harder than its distance alone suggests. A model whose error outgrows its intervals can still be useful if it may abstain, which we test last.

\subsection{Selective prediction supports first-stage screening}\label{sec:screening}

On the 465 blind records, abstaining on the half of the compositions that lie farthest from the training data lowered the MAE from 103 to 71~K and raised coverage to 0.93; abstaining on the half with the widest intervals gave a similar error (73~K) at a coverage of 0.88 (Fig.~\ref{fig:screen}a; Supplementary Table~22). The intervals of the two rules overlap, so we do not rank them. Inside the training chemistry the two rules separate (Fig.~\ref{fig:screen}b; Supplementary Table~23; means over the ten internal test sets): keeping the narrowest half of the intervals lowered the MAE from 103 to 43~K at a coverage of 0.92, whereas keeping the closest half lowered it only to 86~K, because internal test materials are rarely far from the training data. For a room-temperature screen (\Tc{} above 300~K; base rate 0.57), the point prediction had a recall of 0.94 and a precision of 0.77 on the blind records. Deciding only when the 90\% interval lay wholly above or wholly below 300~K settled 37\% of the blind records with an accuracy of 92.5\%, and 44\% of the internal test records with 97.6\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:screen}c; worked examples in Supplementary Table~24). Selective prediction thus turns the model into a first filter that settles part of the candidates and passes the rest on unresolved. The intervals are too wide to rank candidates within one family, and none of these results demonstrates the discovery of a new magnetic material.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The central finding of this study is that the reliability of machine-learning predictions for magnetic materials depends on three conditions that accuracy within the training distribution does not reveal: the physical consistency and provenance of the labels, the information that physical descriptors genuinely add and the chemical domain of application. Ordering labels from DFT and from experiment encode different conventions for the same compounds, and a model trained on one source learns its convention; calibration fails for antiferromagnets and ferrimagnets, the classes on which the conventions differ most. Structural and electronic DFT descriptors describe a computed, mostly ferromagnetic polymorph and add little to composition, and their one clear benefit, for the computed moment, largely reflects cell size. Away from familiar chemistry, error grows faster than the uncertainty estimates. These links are a synthesis of separate measurements, not a demonstration of one mechanism (Fig.~\ref{fig:summary}).

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig6.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Summary: what was observed, what it means and what follows.} For each of the four findings (label provenance, added physics, calibration and chemical novelty), the observation (left), its interpretation (middle) and the practice it suggests for model builders and data providers (right). Text only; the numbers are those given in Results.}\label{fig:summary}
\end{figure}


Why are physically meaningful descriptors not more predictive? Two explanations are supported by our analyses. Composition already fixes much of what the scalar descriptors encode, so they are used heavily without improving ordering or \Tc{} detectably, and structural size acts as a proxy for the moment. Other explanations remain hypotheses. The descriptors belong to the lowest-energy computed polymorph, not necessarily the measured sample; the same-space-group test found no hidden gain, but only 18\% of records report a space group. Scalar descriptors also say little about exchange interactions and the competition between magnetic configurations, which govern ordering and \Tc{}, and they were computed in a ferromagnetic state that is wrong for many experimental antiferromagnets. Label noise, by contrast, is not the binding limit: the spread between records of one formula is far smaller than the model error. The null result is therefore narrow. It concerns descriptors joined by formula, tabular models and one fixed embedding, and it is not evidence against physics-informed models in general or against networks that learn the coupling between structure and properties end to end from the measured phase.

What does the label disagreement mean physically? Three points are established: the disagreement is large; it persists when both labels refer to the same space group and to bulk, stoichiometric compounds; and measured magnetic structures side with the experimental labels for the collinear compounds that they cover. It is not explained by cells that cannot hold antiferromagnetic order or by magnetic supercells, and only partly by a labelling rule that turns small induced moments into ferrimagnetism. What remains is consistent with restricted searches of magnetic configurations in high-throughput DFT, which start from ferromagnetic states\cite{horton2019ordering}. It is not established that a given DFT label is physically wrong. A calculation can find the lowest-energy state among those tried and still differ from experiment because the true order is non-collinear or depends on temperature, defects or composition. DFT searches that enumerate antiferromagnetic configurations for the disagreeing compounds, as done in benchmarks against measured structures\cite{huebsch2021benchmark}, would separate these causes. For model building the consequence does not depend on the cause: pooling the sources mixes two labelling conventions, so a single ordering accuracy cannot be interpreted without its provenance.

Why do models generalize within familiar chemical families but less across new combinations of elements? A composition model interpolates between training compositions; distance to the nearest training formula measures how far it must reach. The correlation between distance and error is moderate, lies between source papers rather than within them, and does not by itself establish a mechanism. The measure uses element fractions only and ignores structure and processing; the unseen systems are dominated by high-entropy alloys, whose Curie temperatures depend on processing. The evidence on unseen systems rests on 69 records from 18 clusters of papers, so we can say that performance there was much weaker, with wide uncertainty, but not how much skill remains.

Several of these phenomena are known in general. Random splits overstate the ability to extrapolate\cite{meredig2018extrapolation,omee2024ood}, marginal conformal guarantees do not extend to subgroups\cite{vovk2013conditional,angelopoulos2023gentle}, and class weights distort probabilities\cite{saerens2002adjusting}. For magnetic data, the ferromagnetic bias of high-throughput DFT configurations\cite{horton2019ordering} and the imperfect precision of text-mined databases\cite{court2020phase} have been documented before. What this study adds is quantitative evidence specific to magnetic materials: the scale of the disagreement between computed and experimental ordering labels, its persistence after phase matching and in cells that can hold antiferromagnetic order, and its consequence for pooled scores; the limited incremental value of DFT descriptors on identical materials, with a control that explains the one gain by cell size; calibration failures that follow the magnetic classes and the highest \Tc{} values; and the rate at which reliability decays with chemical distance on an external benchmark whose apparent independence was mostly overlap.

For screening and discovery, the results indicate when a prediction is informative. A prediction can be acted on when the composition lies close to the training chemistry, its label convention matches that of the training data and its calibrated interval excludes the decision threshold; such decisions were 92.5--97.6\% accurate here. Otherwise the candidate should go on to computation or measurement. Intervals do not guarantee reliability under distribution shift, since their coverage fell with chemical distance and with a change of label source. Because the candidates of a discovery campaign are unfamiliar by definition, a model for discovery should be judged by how it behaves away from its training data and by whether its uncertainty supports a decision. Database providers could make such judgements possible by recording, for each computed ordering, how the magnetic configurations were searched (the initial configuration, whether antiferromagnetic orderings were enumerated and how many were tried) and, for experimental compilations, how the order was determined, at what temperature and on which sample, with explicit nulls instead of default values.

Several limitations affect the interpretation. Labels and descriptors are matched by formula and, where possible, by space group, but not by magnetic structure or by sample. Computational and experimental data are heterogeneous, the experimental compilations were extracted from the literature automatically, and the external benchmark overlaps heavily with our sources. Genuinely unseen chemical systems are few. Some comparisons rest on a single XGBoost realization, and the magnetic-physics test covers \Tc{} only. We tested formula-joined descriptors with tabular models and not end-to-end graph networks, and no candidate material was confirmed experimentally. The label-noise screen was made from titles with the help of a language model, so its figures are estimates and not verified error rates.

This study changes how a reported accuracy for a magnetic property should be read: as a statement about one labelling convention, one descriptor set and one chemical domain, and not about magnetism in general. It establishes that computed and experimental ordering labels disagree even for the same space group, that structural and electronic DFT descriptors give no detectable gain beyond composition for ordering and \Tc{}, and that calibration and accuracy degrade in the subgroups and chemistries that matter most for discovery. It leaves open why individual labels disagree, whether models that learn from structure end to end overcome the descriptor null, and how much skill remains in new chemical systems. Three directions follow. First, ordering labels derived from measured magnetic structures, as we did for 840 compositions, could be extended so that models are trained and tested against experiment. Second, structure-aware models should be tested with the same matched design. Third, the evaluation should be repeated with new measurements in chemical systems that no database covers.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Data collection and harmonization}
The sources were Materials Project\cite{jain2013materials} (100,000 entries); OQMD\cite{saal2013oqmd,kirklin2015oqmd}, obtained through jarvis-tools; JARVIS-DFT\cite{choudhary2020jarvis} (entries with a moment above 0.05~\muB{}); NEMAD\cite{itani2025nemad}, as the classification sets of its published code release and as its two literature tables of magnetic and of anisotropy properties (the experimental compilations), which were downloaded in bulk from the NEMAD website (www.nemad.org) in June 2026 and were produced by language-model extraction from journal articles, mostly in the \textit{Journal of Magnetism and Magnetic Materials}\cite{zhang2024gptarticleextractor}; and the ChemDataExtractor Curie and N\'eel database of Kelly and Cole\cite{kelly2025curie}. A further NEMAD file without compound names and a web-scraped copy of MAGNDATA\cite{gallego2016magndata,gallego2016magndata2}, whose 2,208 rows carry no chemical formula, contributed no rows; MAGNDATA entered the study only as the structural reference described below. The terms of each source are in Supplementary Table~10. Of the compositions in the two literature tables, 90\% and 35\% also occur in the NEMAD classification sets (Supplementary Table~28). Their \texttt{Experimental} flag is false for 1,207 of the 17,641 experimental ordering rows; excluding these rows changes agreement with Materials Project from 28\% to 26\% and $\kappa$ with MAGNDATA from 0.72 to 0.73. All sources were mapped to one schema of 25 columns. Each property kept its value, unit and measurement temperature, and each row kept the name of its source file. Records were deduplicated on material name and DOI together, so independent measurements of one composition were kept (507,828 rows). Formulas were made canonical with pymatgen\cite{ong2013pymatgen}. We set aside 1,940 records whose `formula' was not chemical, such as trade names and generic templates like ABO$_3$.

\subsection{Descriptors and their availability}
Tier~1 (65 descriptors) holds composition statistics in the spirit of Magpie\cite{ward2016magpie}: element flags and fractions; fractions of transition-metal, rare-earth, actinide and magnetic elements; $s$, $p$, $d$ and $f$ fractions and counts of unpaired, $d$ and $f$ electrons; the valence-electron concentration; means and variances of radius, electronegativity, mass, melting temperature, period and group; and size mismatch and entropies. Tier~2 adds eight oxidation-state statistics from the charge-balancing routine of pymatgen. Tier~3 adds 24 structural and electronic DFT properties, joined by reduced formula from the most stable polymorph: formation energy, energy above the convex hull, band gap and band edges, Fermi level, space group, crystal system, point group, volume and density, elastic moduli, dielectric constants, refractive index and the maximum piezoelectric coefficient. Materials Project was the primary source, and JARVIS supplied elastic and dielectric properties where its coverage was denser; thermal properties collected from AFLOW\cite{curtarolo2012aflow} are not among the descriptors and were not used. Seven properties were excluded by design: the magnetic flag, the number of magnetic sites, the magnetic species, the total magnetization, the magnetization per volume and the DFT ordering, which are close to the labels, and the elastic tensor, which is not a scalar. Four finite values that overflow single precision were set to missing.

Tier~1 and Tier~2 can be computed for any composition before a prediction is made. Tier~3 exists only for formulas that a DFT database has computed, so for an uncharacterized compound it requires a DFT calculation first. For the experimental targets (ordering labels from experiment and \Tc{}), Tier~3 describes the computed polymorph and not necessarily the measured phase or sample; for the moment, Tier~3 comes from the same databases, and for most rows from the same calculation, as the label.

\subsection{Magnetic-physics test}
This test covers \Tc{} only. Crystal structures were retrieved for the same Materials Project entries (4,849 structures). As for the DFT descriptors, such a structure may differ from the phase that was measured. Magnetic ions are the elements of curation rule C1 below, and bridging anions are O, S, Se, Te, N, P, As, F, Cl, Br and I. Ten sublattice descriptors were computed with pymatgen: the fraction and number density of magnetic sites; the shortest and the mean nearest metal--metal (M--M) distance; the number of M neighbours within 1.15 times the nearest M--M distance; the mean nearest metal--anion (M--X) distance and the M coordination (X within 1.2 times the nearest distance); the mean M--X--M angle around each anion over its bonded M neighbours, including periodic images; and the fractions of those angles in the ranges 150--180$^\circ$ and 80--100$^\circ$. The magnetic outputs of DFT were the moment per atom, the fraction of magnetic sites, the magnetization per volume and the magnetic flag. The CHGNet embedding is the 64-dimensional pooled crystal feature of the pretrained model (chgnet 0.4.2), used without further training\cite{deng2023chgnet}. XGBoost results in this test come from one realization.

To test the join of the DFT descriptors to the measured phase, the saved test predictions of the Tier~1 and Tier~3 models were scored separately on the 536 matched records whose source space group equals the space-group number of the computed polymorph and on the 237 whose source space group differs. Source symbols were parsed with fixed rules (a number given with the symbol; the international symbol after normalisation of spaces, macrons and missing bars; four structure-type names; a short list of non-standard settings); 69 symbols could not be parsed and 3,760 records report no space group. The improvement from Tier~1 to Tier~3, averaged over the three algorithms, was computed in each split with at least five test rows in the subset, with the intervals of the main comparison. The claim rule was fixed beforehand: a stable gain on the records with the same space group, larger than on the others, would support a noisy join, and an effect indistinguishable from zero would be reported with its minimum detectable effect (17.9~K here). No model was refitted.

\subsection{Targets and curation}
For ordering, 116 raw labels were mapped to FM, AFM, FiM and NM. Hedged labels such as `ferromagnetic-like' and distinct states such as spin glasses and superparamagnets were excluded. Compensated ferrimagnets were kept as FiM. All JARVIS rows were set aside because their label was a constant written by the collector.

Curie temperatures were converted to kelvin. Placeholder zeros from one source and 54 encoded values were removed. Five rules then removed records: C1, formulas without an element capable of magnetism; C2, single elements that are not ferromagnets; C3, values below 1~K; C4, values above 1,400~K, which exceeds the highest known ferromagnetic \Tc{}, that of Co at about 1,388~K\cite{coey2010magnetism}; and C5, formulas labelled only AFM in every source. These rules are screens with known false positives; C1, for example, removes the ferromagnet YTiO$_3$. A sensitivity target that keeps these formulas (30,059 records) gave Tier~1 test errors of 81.2~K for gradient boosting and 72.7~K for XGBoost, compared with 76.2 and 75.4~K, and $R^2$ of 0.77--0.79; the 42 test rows that only rule C5 removes were not harder (XGBoost 54.8~K, compared with 72.9~K for the other rows). The rules were written after the analyses of an earlier version of the dataset had been seen and before any model was fitted to the curated target.

For the title-based screen we drew a random sample of 200 curated records, stratified by source and by \Tc{} tercile (seed 20261110); the three sources supplied 61, 34 and 105 records, in proportion to their shares of the target. From the Crossref title, the compound and the value, and without reading the paper, each record was classified as a magnetic Curie transition, another kind of transition, an implausible value, a computational source, a paper about a different compound, a film or nanoscale sample, or unclear. The categories `computational source', `implausible value' and `different compound' were added during classification. The `different compound' category (16.0\%) is not counted as wrong, because the title of a paper need not name every compound that it reports, which is why we give the range of 11--27\%. The records were classified with the help of a large language model, and the label of every record is released so that it can be checked.

We distinguish four magnetic quantities and never use them interchangeably. The primary moment target \mcell{} is the total moment of the computed cell at 0~K from Materials Project or JARVIS. The moment per formula unit is \mfu{} = \mcell{} $\times$ (atoms per formula unit)/(atoms in the cell), with the atom count of the same calculation (10,821 rows). The moment per atom divides \mcell{} by the number of atoms of the same calculation; an earlier per-atom analysis had used the atom count of a different cell for 25.4\% of rows, and every per-atom value reported here uses the corrected pairing. The experimental saturation magnetization, as mass or volume magnetization, is a secondary target and is not analysed here, nor is the anisotropy constant $K_1$. For the 4,849 Materials Project entries whose records we retrieved, 99\% have energies in the mixed GGA and GGA+U scheme and 1\% in r$^2$SCAN alone, and the recorded orderings are 55\% FM, 27\% unknown, 8\% FiM, 6\% AFM and 4\% NM; in the whole database, 93\% of the magnetic entries were in a ferromagnetic configuration when the ordering was surveyed\cite{horton2019ordering}. We kept \mcell{} as the primary moment target because it is the quantity that the databases report and the one for which the comparisons were configured in advance.

\subsection{Phase-matched comparison of ordering labels}
For the 949 formulas labelled by both Materials Project and an experimental source (modal label per provenance, as in the cross-provenance analysis below), all 1,940 Materials Project entries of the same composition were taken from the raw file used for training, which holds the ordering of every entry; their space-group numbers and energies above the hull were retrieved from the Materials Project API (the recorded orderings were identical to those served by the API). The space groups of the experimental records were parsed with the rules of the space-group test above, unchanged; 682 formulas have at least one parsable experimental space group. A formula is phase-matched when an experimental space group equals that of at least one Materials Project entry; the Materials Project label is then that of the matching entry with the lowest energy above the hull, the experimental label is the modal label of the matching records, and, where several space groups match, the one with the most experimental records is used (330 formulas). Bulk records exclude films, nanostructures, wires, particles and multilayers (by material type and structure description), and stoichiometric formulas have integer subscripts. Agreement and Cohen's $\kappa$ have formula-bootstrap intervals (1,000 resamples). The claim rule was written before the analysis: if the phase-matched subset has at least 30 formulas and an upper $\kappa$ limit below 0.40, the disagreement persists among phase-matched records. A space group does not fix the magnetic cell, the composition of a sample or the temperature of a measurement, so this is a matching at the level of the crystal structure only. Given the class balances, $\kappa$ could not exceed 0.22 in the phase-matched subset, so the rule was lenient; the informative comparisons are the directional rates and the contrast with phase-mismatched formulas (experimental space group parsed but equal to no Materials Project entry; formula bootstrap of the difference). For the decomposition (Supplementary Table~25), each formula is represented by its Materials Project entry with a known ordering and the lowest energy above the hull; the number of magnetic sites and the site moments come from the Materials Project magnetism documents; the FiM recount keeps only sites with $|m|\ge0.5$~\muB{}; and the propagation vector of each MAGNDATA structure is read from its \texttt{\_parent\_propagation\_vector.kxkykz} entries (1,126 of 2,488 structures have $\mathbf{k}\neq0$). Modal labels were tied for 23 experimental and no Materials Project formulas.

\subsection{Splits, models and selection}
Records were split 70/15/15 by reduced formula, with a stratified assignment of groups that balances the sizes of the parts. Robustness analyses used ten splits with prespecified seeds ($20267000+i$), and the leave-chemical-system-out splits grouped records by their set of elements. The models were multinomial logistic or ridge regression with median imputation and standardization, histogram gradient boosting\cite{pedregosa2011sklearn,ke2017lightgbm} and XGBoost\cite{chen2016xgboost}; classifiers used balanced class weights. Hyperparameters were chosen on the validation macro-F1 (classification) or root-mean-square error (regression); the model was then refitted on the training and validation data and scored once on the test set. The grids were $C\in\{0.1,1,10\}$ or $\alpha\in\{0.1,1,10,100\}$ for the linear models; 500 iterations, a learning rate in $\{0.05,0.1\}$ and leaf nodes in $\{31,127\}$ for gradient boosting; and 500 trees, a depth in $\{6,10\}$, a learning rate in $\{0.05,0.1\}$ and subsample and column fractions of 0.8 for XGBoost. On the full populations, histogram gradient boosting reached an ordering macro-F1 of 0.720 and XGBoost a \Tc{} MAE of 75.4~K. Other ordering scores come from other setups: 0.692 is the mean of Tier~1 XGBoost over ten splits (Supplementary Table~7), and 0.712 is the formula-grouped value of the leave-chemical-system-out comparison. To test whether the small grids limited the Tier~3 result, histogram gradient boosting was repeated on the matched \Tc{} population with 64 candidate settings (the original four and 60 random draws over the learning rate, the number of leaves, the minimum leaf size, the L2 penalty, feature subsampling and the number of iterations), the same for both tiers; selection and inference were unchanged. The test errors of both tiers moved by less than 2~K, although the validation error of the selected settings fell by 27 and 34~K, and the formula-bootstrap interval of the tuned contrast ($-21.9$ to $-0.04$~K) makes it a borderline loss. XGBoost was not repeated.

XGBoost results depend on the number of threads, which acts like a random seed: between settings the mean prediction changes by about 25\% of the MAE, the same as between seeds at a fixed thread count (23--28\% of the MAE for the moment), so it reflects the random row and column subsampling and not an instability; histogram gradient boosting did not change at all. Tier results for \Tc{} and \mcell{} are therefore averaged over eight realizations that differ in thread count and seed. The XGBoost tier results for ordering, for \mfu{} and for the site-count control come from one realization; gradient boosting, which does not depend on the thread count, gives the same conclusions. Ridge regression without clipping produced exploded Tier~3 predictions from a few non-physical inputs (an elastic-anisotropy ratio of order $10^{110}$), so the ridge results for \Tc{} use inputs clipped to the 1st and 99th percentiles of the training fold.

\subsection{Statistics}
Tier differences were computed on identical test materials. For each contrast we report the mean over the ten splits and two 95\% intervals: a pooled bootstrap over formulas (1,000 resamples, with the same draws for both tiers), and the corrected resampled-$t$ interval of Nadeau and Bengio, whose variance is inflated by $1/J+n_\mathrm{test}/n_\mathrm{train}$ to allow for the overlap between the $J$ splits\cite{nadeau2003inference}. Tests are two-sided. A contrast is stable if both intervals exclude zero and at least seven of the ten splits agree in sign, and borderline if only one interval excludes zero. The nine contrasts between Tier~1 and Tier~3 form the primary family and are Holm-corrected\cite{holm1979simple}; the ridge member for \Tc{} is the contrast without clipping, and using the clipped contrast gives an adjusted $P$ of 0.74 for it and leaves the other eight unchanged. All other contrasts are secondary and unadjusted. The pooled contrast of Table~\ref{tab:tiers} averages, in each split, the Tier~1 to Tier~3 improvements of the three algorithms (clipped ridge regression for \Tc{}); its refit-aware interval uses the split-to-split spread of that mean, and its bootstrap interval uses formula draws shared by the algorithms. The effect of Tier~3 beyond the site count for \mcell{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:rq1}c) uses the refit-aware interval of the per-split improvements. Practical margins were fixed before the corresponding analyses: 10~K for \Tc{}, 0.02 for macro-F1 and 0.10~\muB{} per cell. The minimum detectable effect is stated at 80\% power; for the contrast between Tier~1 and Tier~3 it was 39~K (gradient boosting), 56~K (XGBoost) and 27~K (clipped ridge regression) for \Tc{}, and 0.040--0.091 in macro-F1 for ordering. Unless stated otherwise, ranges in parentheses in the main text are 95\% intervals.

For ordering, the change from Tier~1 to Tier~2 under gradient boosting ($-0.028$) was a borderline loss. For \mfu{}, the Tier~3 gains were $+0.023$ for XGBoost and $+0.053$ for ridge regression; beyond the site count they were $+0.014$ ($-0.001$ to $+0.028$; positive in nine of ten splits) for gradient boosting, $+0.004$ ($-0.026$ to $+0.033$) for XGBoost and a stable $+0.030$ ($+0.010$ to $+0.051$) for ridge regression. The per-atom gains had intervals across splits of 1.5--4.4\% (gradient boosting), 1.6--3.9\% (XGBoost) and 5.1--6.8\% (ridge regression); analyses that added one descriptor family at a time attributed them mainly to other outputs of the same DFT calculation that produced the moment, and less to the volume descriptors. For \Tc{}, the formula-weighted contrast averages the absolute errors within each test formula before averaging over formulas, with the refit-aware interval; the noise ceiling predicts each test record by the median of the other test records of its formula (formulas with at least two test records, about 660 rows per split). TreeSHAP rankings were stable over ten splits for ordering and the moment (mean pairwise Spearman correlation 0.96--0.98) but less so for \Tc{} (mean 0.91--0.94, minimum 0.83), so for \Tc{} we make statements about descriptor families only.

\subsection{Planning and deviations}
The tier comparisons on ten splits were configured before their models were fitted. The later analyses were planned in writing before they were run, and the plan with its log of deviations is released with the reproducibility package. Six planned analyses were dropped for run time before any of their results were seen: the magnetic-physics test for ordering and for the moment; a \Tc{} comparison restricted to records with the same space group as the computed polymorph (run later, as described above); a matched leave-chemical-system-out comparison; averaging over seeds for the ordering XGBoost models; a rerun of the moment intervals with per-row output; and a refit for the ordering decomposition, whose quantities were taken from saved probabilities instead. Twelve analyses were added after the main analyses in response to review comments, each specified in writing before it was run, and all but the last use saved predictions or data and refit nothing: the structural reference from MAGNDATA, the pooled contrast across algorithms, the screening on the internal test sets, a descriptive check of the cell size of the moment data, the same-space-group test of the descriptor join, the phase-matched comparison of ordering labels, the provenance checks of the experimental labels, the decomposition of the label disagreement, the formula-weighted descriptor contrast and noise ceiling, the cluster jackknife, the re-scoring against measured structures, and the larger hyperparameter search for gradient boosting on \Tc{}. When each reported analysis was specified, and what happened to it, is listed in Supplementary Tables~13 and 14.

\subsection{Uncertainty quantification}
For regression intervals, whole formulas (20\% of the training formulas) formed the calibration set, and models were refitted on the remaining 80\%. Split conformal prediction used absolute residuals. Normalized conformal prediction divided them by a difficulty model fitted to cross-fitted absolute residuals. Conformalized quantile regression used XGBoost models for the 5\% and 95\% quantiles. The conformal guarantee holds when the calibration and test units are exchangeable. Because calibration and test sets are drawn by formula, the exchangeable unit is the formula group, and the guarantee concerns test formulas drawn like the calibration formulas from the same pool; it does not extend to another label source, to unseen chemical systems or to the external table, where we report observed coverage instead. Repeated records of one formula are dependent: the design effect $1+(\bar m-1)\rho$, where $\rho$ is the within-formula intraclass correlation of the indicator that a calibration row exceeds its interval and $\bar m$ is the size-weighted number of records per formula, was 12--17, which implies a standard deviation of coverage between splits of 1.6--1.9 points. This accounts for much of the variability of the normalized method (2.5 points) and for little of that of the other two (about 6 points). Subgroups for evaluation were defined without the difficulty score of any method; Supplementary Table~11 lists every subgroup that we evaluated for \Tc{}, and the moment subgroups come from an earlier run of the same code and splits. Distance-aware (Mondrian) conformal prediction calibrates separately within each quartile of calibration distance; its coverage overall was 89.5\%, and in the farthest distance decile it raised the coverage of the normalized method from 88.2\% to 90.3\%. Weighted conformal prediction used a logistic density ratio in $\log(1+d)$\cite{tibshirani2019covariate}; it changed nothing, because the calibration and test sets come from the same pool. The source-shift coverages come from the single frozen split: intervals calibrated on the larger experimental compilation and tested on text-mined rows covered 76.9\% (constant width), 80.3\% (CQR) and 85.1\% (normalized).

For ordering, probabilities were corrected to the training prior\cite{saerens2002adjusting} and temperature-scaled on half of the calibration formulas. Marginal and class-conditional thresholds on $1-p_\mathrm{true}$ were set on the other half\cite{vovk2013conditional}; fitting both on the same half changed coverage by less than 0.3 points. The expected calibration error (ECE) is reported for the top label and classwise\cite{kull2019dirichlet}, with 15 bins of equal width. The classwise error changed much less than the top-label error (0.035 raw, 0.049 after prior correction, 0.032 with temperature scaling), so temperature scaling repairs the confidence and not the per-class probabilities. On the frozen split, marginal sets calibrated on Materials Project labels covered 83.5\% of experimental labels. For the re-scoring against measured structures, the saved test probabilities of the ten splits (Tier~1 XGBoost, full population) were restricted to test formulas whose composition has a MAGNDATA-derived label, and the argmax class was compared with the dataset label of the row and with the MAGNDATA label (Supplementary Table~29).

\subsection{Cross-provenance ordering}
For formulas labelled by both Materials Project and an experimental source, we compared the most frequent label of each provenance (Cohen's $\kappa$, bootstrap over formulas; the full cross-tabulation is in Supplementary Table~12). For transfer, NM was removed everywhere, because the experimental labels contain only five NM rows. Models were trained on one provenance, after removal of training formulas that occur in the target test set and with hyperparameters selected on the validation rows of the source, and were tested on the test rows of the target, on the frozen split. The values in Results use raw probabilities. With probabilities corrected to the class prior of the target domain, estimated from its training partition, the balanced accuracies were 0.314 (0.247--0.381) from Materials Project to experimental labels and 0.400 (0.370--0.430) in the reverse direction. The null distribution came from 20 refits on permuted training labels; its 97.5th percentiles were 0.374 and 0.351. Our plan allowed the statement that ordering `does not transfer' only if the upper limit of the corrected balanced accuracy lay below the 97.5th percentile of the null. The condition fails narrowly in one direction (0.381 against 0.374) and clearly in the other, so we report the size of the drop and do not make that statement.

\subsection{Structural reference for ordering labels}
We obtained the MAGNDATA repository\cite{gallego2016magndata,gallego2016magndata2,perezmato2015magcryst,tasci2025magtools} from the Bilbao Crystallographic Server\cite{aroyo2011bcs,aroyo2006bcs1,aroyo2006bcs2} through its official download (registered academic account; usage conditions accepted, which require that the server and MAGNDATA are cited): 2,997 file paths, of which 509 were metadata stubs of the archive, leaving 2,488 structure files. Each file was read with pymatgen. Moment vectors were tested for collinearity (sine of the angle to the common axis below 0.02), and collinear structures were labelled FM, AFM or FiM by the collinear magnetic analyser of pymatgen from the signed moments; no label came from text. Of the 2,488 structures, 936 were AFM, 91 FM and 80 FiM; 797 were non-collinear, 397 had disordered sites, 169 were incommensurate and 18 could not be labelled, and these were not used. The 1,107 labelled entries cover 840 compositions, 19 of which have conflicting entries and take their modal label. Compositions were matched to the repository by composition and not by formula string, and the label of each source for a composition was its most frequent ordering label (non-magnetic kept as a fourth label). Agreement and Cohen's $\kappa$ have formula-bootstrap intervals (1,000 resamples). In a check on textbook compounds, all seven that the labelled set contains (MnO, NiO, Cr$_2$O$_3$, LaMnO$_3$, CoO, MnF$_2$ and Fe$_2$O$_3$) came out antiferromagnetic. Where Materials Project and the experimental source disagreed on a composition that MAGNDATA also covers, the experimental label matched the structure in 107 of 114 cases. The reference covers only ordered, collinear structures that have been studied by diffraction, and the experimental sources and MAGNDATA draw on the same literature, so their agreement shows that the experimental labels reproduce published structure determinations and not that the two are independent.

\subsection{Leave-chemical-system-out test}
This test used fixed hyperparameters on ten random splits of the full populations, so its baselines (for example 99~K for \Tc{}) differ from those of the frozen split. The formula-grouped and system-grouped schemes test different materials, so the reported changes combine novelty with differences in the composition of the test sets.

\subsection{External benchmark and audit}
The external table\cite{malaei_magnetic_data,abediorang2026curie} lists 2,500 ferromagnets with experimental \Tc{} and source DOIs, so it says nothing about ferrimagnets or N\'eel temperatures. The audit checks A1--A8 (Supplementary Table~1) were written down before any record-level error was linked to an audit flag or to a distance. They were written after the subset-level results of an earlier analysis were known; that analysis defined novelty by formula string and failed to match DOIs that carried a URL prefix. Three amendments were logged, one of them (clusters defined by the first-listed DOI) after the first bootstrap run. The 105 excluded records comprised 60 computational labels, 18 malformed compositions and 35 values outside our target definition; some records fall under more than one reason.

Novelty is defined by composition identity (spelling-independent element fractions) and by normalized DOIs; records, unique compositions and clusters of source papers are counted separately. Distance is the total-variation distance between vectors of element fractions, taken to the nearest formula among the 23,757 training records. Clusters are records linked by any shared DOI (the prespecified unit), by first-listed DOIs or by chemical systems. Intervals are percentile intervals and bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) intervals from a cluster bootstrap (10,000 resamples); the text quotes the latter. With few clusters (18 for the blind records in unseen systems) both are approximate, and the conclusion does not depend on the interval type (Supplementary Table~4). Skill is also measured against a fixed predictor equal to the training mean (\Tc{} = 338~K); for the records in unseen systems it was $-0.17$ ($-0.77$ to $0.56$). Before analysing the corrected subsets we fixed the wording rule quoted in Results; it, too, was written after the subset results of the earlier, flawed analysis were known, and its threshold of 0.2 is a convention. With chemical systems as the resampling unit, the upper limits are 0.21 for $R^2$ and 0.17 for the fixed-reference skill. The cluster jackknife recomputes MAE and $R^2$ leaving out one cluster at a time; the within-cluster association correlates distance and absolute-error ranks centred within each cluster.

The screening analysis of the 465 blind records was planned in writing. It also gave the precision among the highest-ranked predictions for \Tc{} above 300~K: 1.00 for the top 25 and the top 50 records and 0.93 for the top 100, against a base rate of 0.57. The same rules were applied to the saved per-row test intervals of the ten splits for \Tc{} (Tier~1 XGBoost, 90\% normalized conformal intervals, composition distance to the training formulas); each quantity was computed within a split and averaged over the ten splits, and the standard deviation across splits is descriptive because the test sets overlap. The curves in Fig.~\ref{fig:screen}a use every cut-off of the 465 blind records and are descriptive; the points with intervals are the prespecified cut-offs. The three worked examples are medians over the records of one formula in one internal test set, in which every record of the formula is a test record.

\subsection{Errors found and corrected}
During the study we found and corrected the following errors in our own work: a default `ferromagnetic' label written by the collector on all JARVIS entries (16\% of an earlier ordering target); transitions other than Curie transitions in the \Tc{} column; per-atom moments divided by the atom count of a different cell (25.4\% of rows); an external `blind' test that compared formula strings (1,302 records claimed blind, 502 by composition); a DOI normalizer that missed the \texttt{https://doi.org/} prefix; and a Tier~2 gain for ordering on a single split that did not replicate. In later rounds of checking we corrected a stability rule that was described more loosely than it was implemented, a difficulty subgroup defined by the method under test, a three-class chance reference for a four-class predictor, counts of papers reported in place of resampling clusters, and a wrong citation for the text-mined database. Earlier results are kept unchanged, and each correction is recorded in a versioned register of claims.
